## Passage A — Why First Impressions Matter

A. People often form initial opinions about others after only a brief interaction. Appearance, tone of voice, and behaviour may influence these early judgments. First impressions can help people respond quickly in unfamiliar social situations. However, limited information also creates opportunities for misunderstanding. Someone who speaks very little during a meeting might be considered unfriendly, although they may simply be concentrating or feeling nervous.

B. Once an impression has formed, people may pay greater attention to information that supports it. If someone believes a colleague is unreliable, a missed deadline may seem especially important, while examples of dependable work receive less attention. This tendency can make an early judgment difficult to change, even when additional evidence becomes available.

C. Recognizing the limitations of first impressions can encourage more careful evaluation. Rather than assuming that one behaviour reveals a person's character, individuals can consider alternative explanations and observe patterns over time. First impressions may provide useful starting points, but they should remain open to revision.

## Passage B — The Psychology of Motivation

A. Motivation helps explain why people begin activities and continue them despite difficulties. Some actions are driven by external rewards, such as money, grades, or public recognition. Others are connected to personal interest or satisfaction. These influences are not necessarily opposites. A student may enjoy learning a subject while also wanting to achieve a high examination score.

B. The relationship between rewards and motivation can be complicated. An external reward may encourage someone to begin an unfamiliar task. However, if attention becomes focused entirely on the reward, the person may pay less attention to the activity itself. This does not happen in every situation, and the effect depends partly on how the reward is presented and understood.

C. People may also remain motivated when they can see progress towards meaningful goals. Breaking a difficult project into smaller stages can make improvement easier to notice. Nevertheless, progress alone does not guarantee continued effort. Personal circumstances, available resources, and the value attached to a goal can all influence whether someone continues.

## Passage C — Why People Avoid Difficult Choices

A. Having several options can be useful because it allows people to compare alternatives. Yet an increasing number of choices may also make decisions more demanding. When products or opportunities differ across many features, people may struggle to determine which characteristics matter most. As a result, some delay making a decision even when several reasonable options are available.

B. Fear of regret can add to this difficulty. Someone choosing between two training courses may worry that selecting one means losing the benefits of the other. The person might continue searching for information, hoping to eliminate uncertainty completely. However, additional information does not always produce a clear answer, particularly when the options involve different advantages.

C. One possible response is to establish priorities before comparing alternatives. Deciding which features are essential can make the process more manageable. People may also benefit from accepting that a reasonable choice does not have to be perfect. This approach does not remove uncertainty, but it can prevent endless comparison from replacing practical action.

- Inference: Rút ra kết luận hợp lý từ bằng chứng, không thêm giả định.
- Identifying Main Ideas: Phân biệt luận điểm bao quát với một ví dụ hoặc chi tiết.
- Author's Purpose: Hiểu tác giả đưa ví dụ để giải thích, đối chiếu hay làm rõ giới hạn của một nhận định.
- Evidence vs Assumption: Phân biệt điều passage xác nhận với điều chỉ có vẻ hợp lý.
- True/False/Not Given: Không biến việc thiếu bằng chứng thành kết luận sai.
